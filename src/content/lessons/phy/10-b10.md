title: Throughput math you can do on a whiteboard
source: research
points:
- '<b>The official NR formula</b> (TS 38.306): <code>rate = 10<sup>−6</sup> · Σ v · Qm · f · Rmax · (N_PRB · 12) / Ts<sup>μ</sup> · (1 − OH)</code>.'
- Rmax = 948/1024 (≈ 0.93). Ts<sup>μ</sup> = 10<sup>−3</sup>/(14·2<sup>μ</sup>) is the average symbol time. Overhead OH = 0.14 for FR1 downlink, 0.18 FR2 downlink, 0.08 FR1 uplink, 0.10 FR2 uplink. f is a capability scaling factor (1, 0.8, 0.75 or 0.4).
- '<b>Example:</b> 100 MHz at 30 kHz (273 PRBs), 4 layers, 256QAM: about <b>2.34 Gbps</b>.'
- '<b>Example:</b> 5 MHz at 15 kHz (25 PRBs): 2 layers 64QAM about 40 Mbps; 1 layer QPSK at the top code rate about 6.7 Mbps. At low SINR the real code rate is far lower.'
- '<b>LTE:</b> 20 MHz is 100 × 12 × 14 × 1000 = 16.8 million REs per second. Category 4 is defined as up to 150752 bits per 1 ms TTI with 2 layers: the well-known 150 Mbps.'
- '<i>In practice:</i> uplink is usually the limit: 0.2 W, and the UE often gets few RBs so it can concentrate power.'
say: I estimate with the 38.306 formula from PRBs, modulation, layers and overhead, then compare with what we measure.
trap: Peak rate is a ceiling for one UE with every RB. Cell throughput is shared, so per-user rate depends on how many users share the beam.
