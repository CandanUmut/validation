title: 'The physics: distance, speed, delay, Doppler'
source: research
points:
- <i>Calculation:</i> a low Earth orbit satellite moves at about <b>7.7 km/s at 350 km</b> altitude and <b>7.6 km/s at 550 km</b> (v = √(GM/(R+h))).
- <i>Calculation:</i> <b>delay:</b> at 550 km the one-way delay is 1.8 ms straight overhead, 3.3 ms at 30° elevation and about <b>6 ms at 10°</b> (slant range about 1,800 km).
- <b>Delay changes fast:</b> as the satellite passes, the path length changes by kilometres per second, so the timing advance must keep following it.
- <i>Calculation:</i> <b>Doppler</b> = (line-of-sight speed / c) × f. Only the line-of-sight part counts; it peaks near the horizon at v·R/(R+h), about 7.0 km/s. At 1.9 GHz that is about <b>44 kHz</b>, nearly three LTE subcarriers.
- <b>Doppler rate:</b> the shift sweeps from positive to negative during a pass, fastest when the satellite is overhead.
- '<i>Calculation:</i> <b>free-space path loss</b> = 32.45 + 20·log10(km) + 20·log10(MHz): 153 dB at 550 km overhead and 163 dB at 10° elevation (1.9 GHz), against about 112 dB for a 5 km terrestrial link.'
- <b>Phone limits:</b> 23 dBm (power class 3) and a low-gain antenna, so the satellite side must supply large antenna gain to close the uplink.
say: Against a tower, a LEO link has 40–50 dB more path loss, a few milliseconds of fast-changing delay, and Doppler of tens of kHz.
trap: Do not say 'latency is the main problem'. For LEO the delay is small; the changing delay, the Doppler and the link budget are the hard parts.
